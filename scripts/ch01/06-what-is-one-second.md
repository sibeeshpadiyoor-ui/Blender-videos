- **One idea:** a good clock needs something that repeats very steadily; today the second is defined by the light from a cesium atom.

## Notes for the reviewer (not narrated)
- Challenge answer, revealed at the start of Video 7: one nanosecond is 10⁻⁹ s, so 1 s = 10⁹ ns, one billion nanoseconds. It also links back to Video 3 (light travels about 30 cm in one nanosecond).
- Video 5's challenge answer used here: 1 mm = 10⁻³ m and 1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m, so 1 mm = 1000 μm.
- From the book: the two questions a time standard must answer, Earth's rotation as an old standard, tides and winds changing the length of a day, the 1967 cesium definition, and the 6000-year figure. The book says "in principle" for the 6000 years; the narration keeps the claim simple, and the on-screen text can add the words "in principle".
- The book's Figure 1-2 shows day-length changes of about 3 ms over four years; the video says "a few thousandths of a second".
- The mass video (Video 7) follows the chapter plan: the kilogram and density.
